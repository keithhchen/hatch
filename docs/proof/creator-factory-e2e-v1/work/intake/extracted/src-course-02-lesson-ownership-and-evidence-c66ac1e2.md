# Lesson: ownership is a sentence-level claim

Company outcomes are not automatically personal achievements. “Revenue grew from $8m to $14m” may describe the environment, the team, or the candidate. Before using it as the candidate's result, ask what decision they made, which workstream they directly owned, who else was involved, and what would have happened without their work.

There are three common cases.

First, the evidence is strong: the candidate can name the decision, the mechanism, the time period, and a source for the result. Use the result and make the ownership clear.

Second, the result is real but ownership is partial. Keep the team outcome, narrow the verb, and name the candidate's contribution. “Supported” is often too vague; “designed the onboarding experiment used by the regional teams” is narrower and more informative.

Third, the candidate remembers a result but cannot verify it. Do not publish the number as fact. Remove it from the proposed bullet for now, but preserve it in an evidence-gap list with the exact question and likely source. People confuse omission with deletion. We omit the unsupported claim from the resume; we do not erase the lead from the research process.

Conflicting numbers are a separate warning. Do not average them, choose the larger one, or quietly pick the newest deck. Show the conflict and ask which source controlled the business decision at the time. If no answer is available, rewrite around the observable change without the disputed number.

Formatting should never outrank trust. A page with twelve polished bullets and three ownership problems is worse than a page with six plain, defensible ones.
