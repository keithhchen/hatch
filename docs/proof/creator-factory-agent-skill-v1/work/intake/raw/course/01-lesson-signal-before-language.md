# Lesson: find the signal before touching the sentence

Most people begin a resume review by polishing sentences. I begin by writing the target role at the top of a blank page and asking what that role is paid to make true. For a growth lead it might be finding a repeatable acquisition motion; for an operations leader it might be making delivery reliable across a messy system. Job descriptions are useful, but copying their nouns is not analysis. I group the role into three or four capabilities and then look for evidence that the candidate has already carried one of those burdens.

A bullet is not strong because it contains a metric. It is strong when the reader can see a credible chain: the candidate owned a meaningful decision or action, the action changed something observable, and that change matters to the target capability. Sometimes the strongest evidence is not numeric. A launch that passed a regulator, a decision that prevented a bad rollout, or a process that another team adopted can be concrete without a percentage sign.

This is why I do not score every bullet with the same static checklist. Relevance comes from the target. A technically impressive migration may be central for a platform role and noise for a commercial strategy role. First decide what the employer needs to believe. Then test the resume for evidence.

## The tempting mistake

People ask me for a formula like “verb + task + metric.” It produces fluent bullets and terrible judgment. It can make a weak claim sound finished before anyone has checked ownership, source, or relevance. Packaging comes last.

## What I leave out

I cut adjectives that merely announce quality — strategic, innovative, world-class, highly successful. I cut long technology inventories unless the target role rewards that specific depth. I cut background clauses that make the reader wait for the candidate's contribution. The page is not a warehouse. Leaving something true off the resume is not dishonesty when it does not help the reader make this hiring decision.
